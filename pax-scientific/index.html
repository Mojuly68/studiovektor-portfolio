Optimizing natural flow

Innovative technology

Fans, mixers, pumps, turbines and propellers, and the hulls and fuselages that move through fluid, all fight the same losses: drag, wasted energy, noise and wear. PAX's Streamlining Principle borrows the geometry nature uses to move fluid and builds it into equipment.

Where the geometry comes from

“Conventional technology creates shapes to induce flow. PAX technology employs flow to create shapes.”

Most equipment is designed shape-first: draw a blade, then push fluid with it. PAX works the other way round. Fluids already prefer certain paths, the spirals seen in whirlpools, shells and growing plants. Design the surface along that path and the fluid stops fighting the machine.

The same idea applies whether the fluid is water in a reservoir, air in a server rack or coolant in a heat exchanger.

Interactive · Salinity lab

Why salinity limits membranes but not distillation.

Reverse osmosis has to push water through a membrane against osmotic pressure, and that pressure rises with salt content. Past a point the pumps and membranes can't keep up. A thermal process boils water off the brine instead, so salinity barely changes the job.

PAX Water Technologies

Ensuring water quality

Treated water can go bad while it waits in storage tanks. PAX founded PAX Water Technologies to fix that with the PAX Water Mixer, whose Lily impeller blends fresh inflow through the whole tank.

PAX Water Mixer site ↗
Why stored water degrades

Three causes, one result.

In a thermally isolated top layer, water ages and can lose its disinfectant residual completely. That residual is often the last barrier against microbes and harmful bacteria.

01

Thermal stratification

Sun-warmed water floats on cooler inflow and stops mixing with it.

02

Underutilization

Tanks sized for peak demand turn over slowly the rest of the year.

03

Inlet and outlet layout

When inlet and outlet sit low, new water short-circuits past the upper volume.

Small efficiency gains, cubed.

      Home/About/Flow
      A fascination with flow

      The Streamlining Principle

      Scientists, philosophers and artists have studied flow for centuries. Jay Harman asked a practical question: if fluids prefer certain paths, why not build equipment that follows them? His answer, the Streamlining Principle, turns nature's flow efficiencies into design geometry.

      PAX calls this biomimicry, a term popularized by author Janine Benyus: deliberately copying solutions that nature has already tested. Harman first applied it to boats, producing the award-winning Goggleboat and WildThing watercraft.

      He founded PAX Scientific in 1997 to bring the same efficiencies to fluid-handling equipment: fans, mixers, pumps, turbines and heat exchangers.

      Our mission

      Translate nature's efficiencies into technology, so every industry gets tools that use less energy and material and perform better.

      Why plants pick 137.5°.

      Sunflowers place each seed a fixed angle from the last. Only the golden angle packs them without gaps or spokes. Move the slider a fraction of a degree and the pattern breaks.
